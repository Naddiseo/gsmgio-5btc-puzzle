The GSMG.IO 5 BTC puzzle

In April 2019 someone locked 5 bitcoin behind a grid of coloured squares. Seven years on, the community has peeled back most of the layers. The last ones are still shut. This is a visual map of what is publicly known, and the small pieces of math that tie it together.

Built from the community notes in Naddiseo/gsmgio-5btc-puzzle, current to September 2026.

The shape of the puzzle

The prize sits at 1GSMG1JC9wtdSwfwApgj2xcmJPAwx7prBe. The creator promised to halve it at each Bitcoin halving, and did. In May 2020 half went to 17ucy1K9ZUAaoY6JVtM932W9jUp5LXfyHa, an address that now shows 3.75 BTC and may be a second prize. After the April 2024 halving, about 1.25 BTC remains at the original address.

There are two ways in, and both start from the same image. The first door runs through five phases of ciphers and pop-culture references, and the community had followed it to a wall by the end of 2019. The second door, found only after official hints, leads to a page called SalPhaseIon and a stage called Cosmic Duality. Blue is solved, yellow partly, red still open. Select a stage to jump to it.

Phase 0: Follow the white rabbit

The first hint was follow the white rabbit. The image is a 14 × 14 grid of white and black squares with some blue and yellow ones scattered through it. Treat it as bits, white = 0 and black = 1, and read it the way the rabbit runs: start at the top-left corner and spiral inward counter-clockwise, eight squares to a byte.

Two patterns fall out. Every byte starts with 0, which is the fingerprint of ASCII text. And the coloured squares are not scattered at random: every one of them lands on the last bit of a byte. That is why their values could be pinned down by trial. With blue = 1 the first four bytes read gsmg, and with yellow = 0 the next three read .io. The whole thing spells gsmg.io/theseedisplanted.

A consequence worth knowing: there are 15 blue squares and 9 yellow ones. Since each coloured square is a character's lowest bit, those counts only restate how many characters in the URL have odd or even codes. A 2020 hint says Yellow has a number and so does Blue, but a note in the repository's unverified folder argues these particular counts are not a new input. Nobody has shown what the hint does point to.

Phase 1: The Warning

The URL from Phase 0 opens a page with eight small pictures and nothing to click. Each picture carries a fragment of text, and the image file names give the game away: every blue or black tile has a red partner. Stack each pair and the fragments join into words.

Search for those words, dropping crypto, and one result stands out: Logic's song The Warning, whose verses are headed Phase one, Phase two and Phase 3. The page's own address, theseedisplanted, is the opening of the first verse, and the + − tile stands for the opposites that finish that line.

The page source shows the actual lock: a form wrapped in display: none that posts one field, password, to /phase1verification. The page is Phase one, so the password is the opening line of Phase two, lowercased and joined up. It's a lyric, so it's blanked out here. Its shape is enough to follow the step.

The redirect lands on a URL made of two lines spoken by the Merovingian in The Matrix Reloaded. His signature idea, causality, is the first part of the Phase 2 password. Two habits are set here for the rest of the puzzle: clues often need a search engine, and passwords are lower case with no spaces unless a hint says otherwise.

Phase 2: A seven-part key

Phase 2 is an AES-encrypted blob whose password has seven parts, each a reference you had to recognise. Joined together they make a 227-character string, and its SHA-256 digest opens the next blob. That pattern of recognise, concatenate, hash, decrypt repeats for the rest of the first door.

Part 5 is the scar in this phase. The string 11110 was found by brute force, and the intended reasoning has never been published. Part 7 comes from a chess puzzle in which a white piece must be moved without delivering checkmate. The whole resulting position goes into the password, written in FEN notation: B5KR/1r5B/2R5/2b1p1p1/2P1k1P1/1p2P2p/1P2P2P/3N1N2 b - - 0 1.

Phase 3: Three riddles

The Phase 2 key opens a page with three short riddles and one more AES blob. The mechanics are the same as before: solve each riddle, glue the answers together, hash, decrypt. The clues are softer, though. Each one is a pointer you follow with a search engine until a name or a phrase falls out.

The first riddle picks up where Phase 1 left off. If the Merovingian is wrong about causality, the alternative is choice, and The Choice Is Ours is a film by The Venus Project, whose founding thinker was Jacque Fresco. The second leans on a line popularly attributed to Alice in Wonderland: asked how long forever is, the White Rabbit answers sometimes, just one second. The riddle says to put giveit in front, and the working password drops the sometimes. The third is close to a textbook definition of Heisenberg's uncertainty principle. The possessive form, heisenbergs, is the variant that works.

Phase 3.2: Three classical layers

The next page opens with a speech in the voice of the Matrix's Architect. Below it sit a block of garbled characters, a long run of digits, an odd phrase about chess pieces, and one more AES blob. One sentence in the speech carries three clues. One for one, four for one names the IBM EBCDIC code page 1141. A beautiful strategic position names the Beaufort cipher. And … am I here? Wake up, you… points to the next line of the film, which is the key: thematrixhasyou.

Layer one: the wrong code page

The garbled block is 1,539 characters drawn from only 26 distinct symbols, which is the first hint that it is a letter cipher in disguise. Every character is a byte shown through the wrong lens. EBCDIC, IBM's mainframe encoding, puts characters at completely different byte values from ASCII. Layer two: Beaufort

Beaufort is subtraction on a clock with 26 positions. Each plaintext letter is the key letter minus the cipher letter, counted mod 26. Because P = K − C and C = K − P have exactly the same shape, encrypting and decrypting are the same operation.

What comes out is a long message in the Architect's voice. It mentions the prime basics near its end, and says the private key must be found by choosing among many ciphers, encryptions and intertwined passwords.

Layer three: the checkerboard

The digits underneath have a lopsided look: more than a third of them are 1s. That is the fingerprint of a straddling checkerboard, the letter-to-digit table at the heart of the Soviet VIC cipher. Eight symbols get a single digit. The other twenty get two, and those always start with one of two reserved digits. One for one, four for one does double duty here as the reserved digits 1 and 4. The alphabet comes from the page's phrase fubcd-king & oracle-queen, thingky mvps: drop the chess pieces, turn the repeated c into a slash, and append the unused letters and a full stop.

The message is a note about who the keys belong to. It decodes cleanly, but it doesn't say what to do next. That leaves the last object on the page, the one that stopped everyone at the end of 2019:

The second door

For months nobody got further. Then the creator posted two hints. In January 2020, a poem: Go back to the first puzzle piece without further ado, with a warning that the rabbit's nest might hold more than one door. In February 2020, a screenshot from the virtual world Decentraland at coordinates −41, −17, where clicking a question mark plays five seconds of hiss.

The hiss is stereo. Both channels carry the same loud noise, but a quieter signal has been added to one channel and subtracted from the other. Add the channels and the secret cancels out. Flip the sign of one channel first and it's the noise that cancels. Move the slider: this is the real audio from the puzzle, and your browser is computing the spectrogram of L + gR as you drag.

Notice how late the text shows up. At g = −0.9 a tenth of the noise survives, and it still drowns the message. Only near-exact cancellation reveals it. The glyphs are hex ASCII codes, and they read HASHTHETEXT.

For Python readers: the community notebook inverts the channel with (-1**i)*x. Exponentiation binds tighter than unary minus, so that is -(1**i)*x, plain negation, which happens to be exactly what the job needed.

The text on the first puzzle piece is its title and the prize address. Hash it, and the digest is a URL.

So gsmg.io/89727c598b…2f6a32 is the way in. Some groups seem to have reached it quietly in late 2020, but the method was only spelled out publicly in May 2021.

SalPhaseIon: One string, several alphabets

The page behind the hash holds an image titled SalPhaseIon Cosmic Duality and 1,075 space-separated letters. The key observation is that different stretches use different alphabets. Colour each run by the letters it uses, and the structure appears.

Two runs use only a and b. That is binary, a = 0 and b = 1, and it reads matrixsumlist and enter. Two runs between z separators use a to i plus o. Put together with the rest of the record, this is where the work stands:

  • Phase 2, part 5. 11110 works, but nobody has published why.
  • The end of door one. The AES blob at the bottom of Phase 3.2. The digits above it decode, but the note they carry doesn't say how to open the blob.
  • SalPhaseIon's a–i runs. 91 and 570 letters in a nine-letter alphabet, plus shabef twice and anstoo.
  • SalPhaseIon's AES blob. The recovered phrases look like a recipe for its password, but no combination published so far decrypts to anything with structure.
  • Cosmic Duality itself. Linked by hints to primes, to a ying yang (the creator says hitting it means solving the rest the same day), and to characters that need to be zeroed out.

Seven years of hints

The creator, posting as Jrk on Telegram, has dropped hints at irregular intervals. Each dot is one, and the line above is the prize. Select a dot to read it.

The two July 2026 messages reframe things. The creator says close friends have the best chance of solving it but lack the skills of some solvers, and adds that the 5 BTC was never the actual prize, only a tiny fraction of it. Whatever else is behind the last door, it seems to be more than a private key.

How to tell a real step from noise

This year the repository's maintainers added a note aimed at people and language models alike: if a decryption produces output with no verifiable structure, it is indistinguishable from failure. That sounds like common sense, but it can be made precise with two tools. Shannon entropy measures how much structure a string has. Plain counting measures how hard you searched to find it. Put them on the same scale, in bits, and most claimed solutions fail on arithmetic alone.

Structure is missing entropy

Entropy measures how unpredictable the next symbol is. If symbol i turns up with frequency pi, the entropy is H = −Σ pi log2 pi bits per symbol. A perfectly flat histogram over an alphabet A reaches the maximum, log2 |A|. Any lopsidedness pulls H below it, and that gap, the redundancy, is what a codebreaker leans on.

A flat histogram doesn't rule out structure one level down. Slice a string into p columns, every p-th symbol in each, and measure the columns. If a repeating key of length p is hiding underneath, each column was shifted by a single key letter, so it stays lopsided and its entropy drops. To tell a real drop from a lucky one, compare against the same symbols shuffled. Every number below is computed in your browser from the puzzle's own data.

English is lopsided, about 11% redundant letter by letter, and the Beaufort layer flattens that to about 1%, which is why the ciphertext looks like noise. Slice it into 15 columns, though, and it collapses. The key thematrixhasyou has 15 letters, and the smaller dips at 3, 5, 10 and 30 are periods that share a factor with 15. The VIC digits are skewed toward 1 before any slicing, which is how solvers guessed a checkerboard.

SalPhaseIon's a–i runs are the frustrating case. Their letter counts are lopsided enough that they are not uniform random digits: b is a quarter of the short run, g almost a fifth of the long one. But no period from 1 to 40 shows anything beyond chance. Whatever structure they carry is not the kind these statistics can reach, so no amount of number-crunching will hand over the method.

Search long enough and words appear

The opposite trap is finding structure that isn't there. Take the 91-letter a–i run and do what the solved runs did: give each letter a digit, read the result as one big decimal number, write it in hex, and read the bytes as text. There are 9! = 362,880 ways to hand out the digits 1 to 9. Your browser tries every one of them, on the real run and on a shuffled copy, and looks for common English words in the output.

Words turn up: a hundred or so three-letter ones and a few four-letter ones, at almost exactly the rate chance predicts, and at the same rate in the shuffled copy. A word pulled out of a big search is not evidence. Compare an output that is. The solved 29-letter run gives thispassword, 12 lower-case bytes out of 12, from the one obvious digit rule. A random 12-byte string manages that about once in 800 billion tries.

Bits of evidence against bits of search

Both ideas fit on one scale. The evidence in an output is −log2 of the chance that a wrong guess would produce something at least as structured. The search is log2 of how many guesses, transforms and settings were tried. If the search is bigger than the evidence, you should expect about 2search − evidence false positives, and your solution is probably one of them. Every real step in this puzzle clears the bar by miles.

Every missing bit doubles the work

The same arithmetic explains why brute force only ever worked in small, well-checked corners, like the five-bit 11110 in Phase 2. When there is no structure to exploit, all that's left is trying keys, and each bit you don't know doubles the number of tries.

Numbers also cut the other way. The 570-letter a–i run holds about 1,807 bits of raw information, so a method with that many free choices could make it say anything at all. That is why a route that needs heavy massaging proves nothing, however good the output looks. The creator has said the remaining steps are easier than the solved ones. A real solution will look like the others: a short, natural rule, and output whose structure no search could have manufactured.

That's worth remembering when a SOLVED announcement appears in the GitHub issues, and several did in 2026. An on-chain check published on 3 September 2026 found the prize address still holding about 1.256 BTC. A real solution will show up as the prize moving.
